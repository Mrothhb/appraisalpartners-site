Appraising is painting a portrait.

I've been doing this long enough to know the data never speaks for itself.

Give the same house to two appraisers with forty years and every designation, and you'll get two values, two sets of reasons, and both can hold up. People outside the profession think that's a flaw.

It's the whole job.

A value opinion is a portrait of how the market sees a property, painted from evidence.

No two artists paint it exactly alike.

Every assignment starts as a blank canvas. I research until a picture forms in my head: the market it competes in, the buyer who'd want it, what the value has to explain.

That picture is the appraisal. Everything after is getting it onto canvas.

And getting it onto canvas used to eat my life. Records, zoning, MLS sheets, photos, maps, one browser tab at a time. Then hours of typing, field by field, photo by photo, holding the whole picture in my head while I filled in its corners.

By the fourth report of the day I wasn't painting anything. I was getting it done.

We've all been there.

That's the part I don't do anymore. The materials show up laid out on one table. The canvas fills while I watch. My picture, whole, in front of me, while I've still got the energy to argue with it.

So I do what painters do. Step back. Swap the comp that looked strong until it sat next to the others. Tighten the reconciliation that isn't earning its number. Paint over a section and look at it fresh. A change costs a sentence instead of twenty minutes, so I make all of them.

I finally get to nitpick my own work. That was always the part of this job I liked.

The last brushstroke never left my hand. The comps, the adjustments, the value, the signature.

Now my desk does the same for other appraisers. You bring the vision and the judgment. We carry the supplies and the brushwork. You step back, direct, and sign.

You're the artist. We're the studio.

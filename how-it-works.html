The process, start to finish

  1. 1.Start with one report. Fill out the short form. You get an order number and a private folder link back, usually within business hours. No call required, and no borrower documents go in the form itself.
  2. 2.Send the packet. Drop the order, photos, sketch, notes, and contract into your folder, or reply to the acknowledgment with attachments. The start page's checklist shows exactly what your kind of assignment needs, so there's no guessing and no retyping.
  3. 3.Get a firm acceptance. Received is not accepted. I check permission, completeness, complexity, scope, software, and my own capacity. Production begins only after you see the accepted scope, the flat price, and the committed return time. If something's missing, the order shows "waiting on your input" and the clock pauses until it arrives.
  4. 4.Follow the report, and steer it. Watch the status page, or get pinged at the milestones. Send corrections, more evidence, or comp direction to the order thread at any point. It all lands in one place.
  5. 5.Review, revise, sign, deliver. The editable draft comes back with the workfile organized behind it, checked twice: against the source data, then read through the way a reviewer would. Accept, change, or reject anything in it. The final judgments, the signature, and the delivery are yours.

Only you have the link, and the page never shows your client's name, the property address, or any number from the report. If the link ever ended up in the wrong hands, all anyone would see is a report moving through stages.

Comps and direction

At intake you pick one of three ways to run the comparables:

  1. 1.Use my comps. You pulled them, you know the market. I build from your selections and your direction.
  2. 2.Prepare candidates and proceed. I research the pool, document what was considered, and build the grid from the strongest candidates. You judge the selections at review.
  3. 3.Candidate memo first. Before the grid is built, you get a short memo: the pool, the candidates, and why. Reply with direction, or let it proceed as proposed after the stated window. Silence lets production continue; it never means you've adopted the comps. That adoption happens at your review, like always.

Steering means facts and evidence: corrections, include-or-exclude direction with your reasoning, additional market data, a documented change in scope. It never means steering toward a number. If an instruction reads like value pressure, I'll say so and decline it.

When something needs fixing

Four kinds of fixes, four plain answers. The rule behind all of them: I fix everything mechanical, and you never type. Your job is judgment.

  • My error, my fix. A transposed number, a mis-mapped field, a missing exhibit, a file that won't validate. Free, first in line, no time limit, no count limit. My errors are mine.
  • Your direction. You reviewed the draft and want it different: swap a comp for your preferred candidate, reweight the reconciliation, reword the commentary. One consolidated round is included with every report. Send it as one list, the way a lender sends conditions, and I confirm a return time before starting.
  • The assignment changed. Amended contract, re-measured GLA, a new intended user. I confirm new timing first, and if it means real rework, you get a quote before anything happens. You can always say no.
  • Your client kicks it back. Underwriter conditions after you've delivered? Send them over. Anything that traces to my production work is fixed free and first in line, for thirty days after delivery. Your underwriter's conditions are my problem too.

And if the draft fundamentally isn't usable to you, not a comp swap but a real miss, you don't pay for it. That's not a first-report promotion. It's the standing rule on every report.

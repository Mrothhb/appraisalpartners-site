Where the practice came from

My father, Tim Roth, MAI, SRA, built the California practice where I trained from trainee through licensure. His standard was simple: define the appraisal problem before opening the form. The form is the delivery vehicle; the appraisal is the analysis behind it.

That standard still shapes my work. I remain accountable for the assignments I accept and the conclusions I sign.

UAD 3.6, in practice now

New form, same responsibility.

The dynamic URAR asks for more structured data and makes the relationships between fields more visible. I'm producing UAD 3.6 reports now, with the research, intake, and review process rebuilt around the new structure.

The form still does not choose the right comparable, recognize what matters about a location, or explain why a conclusion makes sense. That remains the appraiser’s work.

The transition is also why the separate Production Desk exists now: experienced appraisers should be able to keep their work and client relationships moving while they learn the new structure.

Why now

I have a full-time career in engineering and product operations, so I keep my own field practice selective rather than promise a high-volume inspection calendar. That work has also sharpened the side of appraisal I've always liked most: research, analysis, and report writing that holds up to checking. It's also why the desk stays deliberately small: I take only the files I can personally produce and review, up to five standard files per business day, and I've built my process so the review is real, never rushed.

I want a few durable professional relationships with clients and peers who care about credible results. The practice will stay small enough for accountability to remain personal.
